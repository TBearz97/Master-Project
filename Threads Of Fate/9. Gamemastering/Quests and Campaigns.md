Creating adventures for Threads of Fate does not require a massive plot diagram or a perfectly mapped world. Most sessions work best when the party is given a clear problem, a reason to care, and enough freedom to decide how they will solve it.

###### **Quest Design**

Most quests can be built from a simple core objective. Common structures include:

- **Kill or Defeat Something:** Slay a monster, drive off raiders, stop an outlaw, or survive a dangerous threat.
- **Fetch or Recover Something:** Retrieve a relic, escort a person, steal a document, or recover lost supplies.
- **Speak with Someone:** Negotiate peace, secure aid, expose a lie, or persuade an enemy to stand down.
- **Achieve a Task:** Repair a bridge, scout a ruin, seal a breach, chart a route, or complete a ritual.

The strongest quests usually combine at least two of these. A simple rescue becomes more interesting when the rescue target does not want to leave. A monster hunt becomes more memorable when killing the creature is only one of several possible solutions.

###### **Start with the Core Question**

Before building encounters, answer four things:

- what is the problem?
- who wants it solved?
- why has it not already been solved?
- what changes if the party succeeds or fails?

If you know those answers, you usually have enough to run the session.

###### **Quest Givers**

The person offering the work should want something specific. Give them a motive beyond simply handing the party an objective.

Useful questions for a quest giver:

- What do they stand to gain?
- What are they hiding?
- Why do they need the player characters?
- What do they believe is true that may not actually be true?
- What reward can they credibly offer?

Even a small job feels stronger when the quest giver has their own worries, blind spots, and agenda.

###### **Travel and the Road**

Journeys matter when they create pressure, discovery, or choice. Do not turn every trip into filler combat. Use travel when it can reveal the state of the world, wear down supplies, introduce new factions, foreshadow danger, or force the party to choose between speed and safety.

Helpful travel questions include:

- What does this road, forest, pass, or river say about the region?
- What hazard makes the journey memorable?
- What clue or rumor can the party learn on the way?
- What resource or condition might matter before they arrive?

If the journey adds nothing, summarize it and move on.

###### **Flexible Obstacles**

Do not build quests around only one correct solution. Build obstacles instead.

Examples:

- a guarded gate
- a hostile witness
- a collapsing bridge
- a frightened village
- a rival group after the same prize
- a creature that cannot easily be beaten in direct combat

Each obstacle should support multiple solutions. Let the party choose whether they want to persuade, sneak, fight, investigate, bargain, endure, or improvise.

###### **Twists and Pressure**

A good twist does not invalidate the players' choices. It changes what those choices now mean.

Good twists include:

- the employer lied
- the target is not the real villain
- the enemy is already under attack
- the objective must be completed before a deadline
- success for one faction damages another
- the rescued person refuses the party's plan

Use twists to deepen the situation, not to punish the party for engaging with it.

###### **Meaningful Decisions**

The most memorable part of many quests is not the fight, but the choice that follows it. Whenever possible, include at least one decision that matters beyond immediate survival.

Examples:

- uphold a promise or take the better reward
- protect civilians or secure the objective
- back the lawful faction or the sympathetic one
- hand over dangerous knowledge or destroy it
- spare a defeated enemy or end the threat permanently

If you want the party to care, make the consequences visible.

###### **Rewards**

Quest rewards should fit the scale of the problem, the wealth of the quest giver, and the risk the party accepts. Gold is common, but it should not be the only reward.

Useful reward types include:

- gold
- gear
- safe passage
- social access
- faction support
- information
- property, shelter, or transport
- reputation or renown

Not every quest giver can pay well. A poor village might offer food, labor, local loyalty, and future help instead of coin.

###### **Experience Awards**

Your game already grants XP for defeating or overcoming NPCs. Quest XP should support pacing, not replace that system or overwhelm it.

As a rule of thumb, award quest XP once at the end of the objective and keep it modest compared to the XP needed for the next level.

Recommended quest XP per player:

| **Quest Scope** | **XP Award** |
| --- | --- |
| Minor objective or short side task | `Level x 10` |
| Standard session-length quest | `Level x 20` |
| Major quest or major story turning point | `Level x 35` |

This works well because the next level normally costs `current level x 100` XP. That means a standard quest is usually worth about one fifth of a level before NPC XP is counted.

If the party already earned a large amount of NPC XP during the quest, reduce or even skip bonus quest XP. If the quest involved little combat but major risk, travel, diplomacy, or investigation, the quest XP can do more of the progression work.

###### **Obstacle XP**

For major non-combat obstacles, puzzles, negotiations, discoveries, or survival challenges, you may award a small additional XP bonus when the party overcomes something significant.

Recommended bonus XP per player:

| **Obstacle Importance** | **XP Award** |
| --- | --- |
| Meaningful setback or difficult obstacle | `Level x 5` |
| Major scene-defining obstacle | `Level x 10` |

Use these sparingly. If every locked door, clue, and argument gives XP, advancement becomes too fast.

###### **Milestone Play**

If your group uses milestone progression, ignore the exact XP values and instead use this chapter to judge when the party has earned advancement through meaningful accomplishments. In milestone campaigns, the same guidance still helps you judge what counts as a minor objective, a full quest, or a major turning point.

###### **Quest Length**

As a practical default:

- a small job fits inside one focused session
- a standard quest usually lasts one to two sessions
- a major quest can span several sessions and reshape the campaign

Long campaigns are built from these smaller units. Even when you have a grand plot in mind, think in terms of immediate objectives, near-term consequences, and the next hard choice facing the party.

###### **After the Quest**

Give the party time to feel the outcome. Let them report back, collect rewards, ask questions, deal with consequences, and decide what they do next.

The aftermath is where victories become reputation, grudges become future plots, and small decisions grow into campaign history.
